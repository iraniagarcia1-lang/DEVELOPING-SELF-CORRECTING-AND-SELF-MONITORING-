1. The "Carry-Over" Problem

The Core Challenge

What is the "Carry-Over" Problem?

One of the most serious challenges facing English pronunciation teachers in language classrooms today.

"Students achieve near-standard pronunciation during class time, but they revert to their former non-standard patterns as soon as they leave the classroom."

Why Does This Happen? (4 Key Factors)

Motivation

Students may not see why accuracy in pronunciation truly matters for communication.

Sensitivity to Accuracy

Some students prioritize continuous fluency over linguistic accuracy when speaking.

Age

Older learners often face entrenched motor habits and established phonological filters.

Education & Training

Students may simply have never learned how to self-monitor in real time.

Stage 1: Assisted Awareness Dependent on External Feedback

What is Self-Correction?

The ability to correct oneself after a pronunciation error has been pointed out by an instructor or peer.

The 4-Step Self-Correction Process:

1
Notice
Teacher/peer points out error
2
Pause
Student stops briefly
3
Correct
Repeats target sound accurately
4
Continue
Resumes communication
Classroom Scenario Example:
Teacher: "You said 'wiff' — can you try that again?"
Student: "Oh, I said 'wiff' instead of 'with'. Let me try: 'with'."
Teacher: "Perfect!"
🔧

Key Insight: Self-correction is the foundational first step towards full independence.

Stage 2: Autonomous Monitoring Fully Independent

What is Self-Monitoring?

The ability to internalize criteria, listen to oneself, and recognize errors without anyone pointing them out.

The 5-Step Autonomous Cycle:

1
Speak
Produces utterance
2
Listen
Self-monitors output
3
Notice
Catches error solo
4
Correct
Adjusts articulators
5
Continue
Flows naturally
Autonomous Student Thought & Action:

Student speaks: "I have tree brothers..."

Internal Monitor: "Wait, I said 'tree' but I meant 'three'..."

Self-correction: "...I have THREE brothers..."

Continues seamlessly: "...and one sister."

👂

The Ultimate Goal: Self-monitoring enables learners to improve independently in any real-world setting.

Pedagogical Foundations

The Four Key Areas

To develop self-correction & monitoring, teachers must focus on these four pillars (Avery & Ehrlich):

1. Motivation

Students must understand why oral production accuracy is critical. Without intrinsic motivation, effort drops off.

2. Explanations

Provide a balanced mix of description and physical demonstration so students visualize sound formation.

3. Practice

Provide appropriate, varied, and adequate practice opportunities to build muscle memory and vocal confidence.

4. Feedback

Deliver supportive, accurate teacher and peer feedback to guide students toward target sound targets.

🏗️ "These four areas form the essential foundation for building self-correcting learners."
Classroom Tools

6 Self-Correction Techniques

Practical instructional tactics from Avery & Ehrlich (Ch. 14):

1. The "Peg" System

Use simple tactile anchors. e.g., for /w/ vs /v/: /w/ = no teeth touch; /v/ = top teeth touch bottom lip.

2. "Walk" Through Sounds

Guide students step-by-step like a dance: lower teeth to lower lip, tense lip slightly, blow air out.

3. Sound Contrast

Say correct and incorrect versions back-to-back alternately (e.g., "we" / "ve" / "we" / "ve").

4. Code Words

Associate vowels with colors: /iː/ = green, /ɪ/ = pink, /æ/ = black, /ʌ/ = mustard.

5. Letter Articulation

Link shape of letter 'W' (2 rounded lips) vs 'V' (single point touching lower lip).

6. Analogies

Use mental images: shape the tongue like a "spoon" held under the upper front teeth for /θ/ & /ð/.

🎯 "Different techniques work for different learners. Try them all to find what clicks!"
Teacher Shift Strategy

Teacher Feedback & 'Reminding'

Once a student CAN produce the correct sound, stop modeling it directly! Instead, use 'Reminding' techniques to cue them to correct themselves.

1. Questioning Repetition

Rising Intonation

Repeat the student's incorrect sound with a questioning tone.

Teacher: "Wiff a friend? ↗"

2. Query the Word

Question Slot

Replace the mispronounced word with a question word.

Teacher: "He went WHAT a friend?"

3. Exaggerate the Error

Acoustic Highlight

Repeat the error in an exaggerated manner so the flaw is unmistakable.

Teacher: "Wffffff a friend?"
🔄 Goal: Shift responsibility from teacher to student! If error persists after cued attempts, explain source of difficulty.
Progression & Differentiation

Stages & Proficiency Dynamics

3 Stages of Self-Monitoring Mastery:

Stage 1: Production

Attempts target form and evaluates how close output is to target.

Stage 2: Contextualized

Listens to audio samples to identify correct/incorrect forms in context.

Stage 3: Communicative

Anticipates error spots ahead of time during free speech.

Monitoring Focus by Proficiency Level:

Basic Students

Focus on global features: Intonation, Phrasing, Rhythm (these have the greatest overall effect on comprehensibility).

More Proficient Students

Focus on Specific Segments (individual consonant and vowel sound distinctions).

Teacher Tip: Students with long-entrenched errors must first standardly believe accuracy matters and accept personal ownership.
